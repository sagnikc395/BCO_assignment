\section{Part 1}

\subsection{Question}
Run the following code again
\begin{verbatim}
python test_gym.py
\end{verbatim}
What do you notice happening? What specifically about MountainCar makes the
problem difficult for RL?

\subsection{Solution}
The random policy jitters the car back and forth near the bottom of the valley and never
leaves it: every one of the 200 steps returns $-1$ and the episode ends with a return of
$-200.0$, with the car still in the basin.

MountainCar is hard for RL because the reward signal carries no information about
progress. The engine is weaker than gravity, so the only way to reach the flag is to
first drive \emph{away} from it and pump energy into the system over many steps
(a long, precisely correlated sequence of actions). A uniformly random policy has
essentially zero probability of producing such a sequence within 200 steps, so the
agent almost never sees a return other than $-200$. Three consequences follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exploration / sparse-in-practice reward:} all trajectories look equally
        bad ($-200$), so the reward gives no gradient toward the goal. The agent is
        stuck in a flat region of the return landscape, i.e. a local optimum where
        nothing it tries locally looks better than anything else.
  \item \textbf{Long credit-assignment horizon:} the action that matters (backing up the
        left hill) is rewarded only tens of steps later, when the car finally clears the
        right hill.
  \item \textbf{Counter-intuitive optimal behavior:} a greedy/myopic policy that pushes
        toward the goal never succeeds; the optimal policy must temporarily move in the
        wrong direction.
\end{itemize}

I expected the bad demonstration to pull the policy toward always

The training loss is not what fails here. It drops quickly from $0.93$ to about $0.40$
within 30 iterations and then flattens into a slow decline to $0.33$, i.e.\ the network
fits the mixed dataset about as well as it fit the single clean demonstration in Part 3
($0.29$), yet the resulting policy is strictly worse than either demonstrator. Most of the
loss that remains once it levels off (around $0.33$--$0.40$) is \emph{irreducible label
noise}: the two
demonstrations visit overlapping states but label them with different actions, so no
deterministic policy can fit both. Cross-entropy responds by predicting something close to
the state-conditional average of the two behaviors, and because the argmax of that average
is dominated by the ``push right'' action (it is the only action in the bad demo and also
appears throughout the second half of the good one), the policy just pushes right and never
builds momentum.

The bad demonstration also carries more weight than its count suggests: a failed episode
always lasts the full $200$ steps while a successful one ends early, so it contributes $200$
of the $316$ training transitions ($63\%$). Averaging two valid-but-different behaviors gives
behavior that is worse than either, and the low BC loss does not reveal this, since the loss
is measured on the demonstrator's own states, not on the states the learned policy actually
reaches.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Why bad demonstrations are a problem.} BC is pure supervised learning on
$(s,a)$ pairs and has no notion of demonstration quality: every transition contributes
equally to the loss, so a bad demonstrator is imitated just as faithfully as a good one.
Worse, in MountainCar the good and bad policies disagree precisely in the states that
matter (when to switch direction), so the conflict is not averaged away harmlessly, and it
destroys the one behavior that solves the task.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{One idea for robustness.} Weight demonstrations rather than treating them
uniformly: since we can evaluate each demonstration's return in the environment (or its
episode length / whether it reached the flag), fit BC with per-demonstration weights
$w_i \propto \exp(R_i / \beta)$, or simply filter to the top-$k$ / above-median
demonstrations before training. Because bad demos are assumed to be a minority, the median
return is a good threshold and the outliers get (near-)zero weight. If no reward is
available at all, an unsupervised variant works similarly: fit BC, then compute each
transition's loss under the fitted policy and down-weight or drop the demonstrations with
the highest disagreement (a trimmed-loss / RANSAC-style approach), iterating a few times so
that the majority mode wins and the inconsistent minority is discarded.




\section{Part 6}

\subsection{Question}
At a conceptual level, describe what changes you would need to make to the BC
code to implement BCO(0), as described in the paper we read in class:
\url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.01954.pdf}. Answer this question before starting
Part 7.

\subsection{Solution}
BCO(0) removes the assumption that the demonstrations contain actions: we
observe only the state sequences $\{(s_t, s_{t+1})\}$ of the demonstrator and must infer
the actions ourselves before we can run BC. Conceptually there are three changes to the BC
code, and one full pass through them ($\alpha = 0$ means no post-demonstration iteration):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pre-demonstration / self-supervised data collection.} Before touching the
        demonstrations, interact with the environment using a random policy and record
        $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ transitions. Here the actions \emph{are} known because we
        chose them, so this data is free and needs no human. This is what
        \texttt{collect\_random\_interaction\_data} provides.

  \item \textbf{Learn an inverse dynamics model.} Train a new network
        $M_\theta(a \mid s_t, s_{t+1})$ on the random interaction data. Since the action
        space is discrete (3 actions), this is again a classification problem trained with
        cross-entropy, structurally the same training loop as BC, but with input
        $(s_t, s_{t+1})$ (i.e.\ 4 input features instead of 2) and the executed action as
        the label. This model is trained \emph{only} on the self-generated data, never on
        the demonstrations.

  \item \textbf{Label the demonstrations, then clone.} Discard the demonstrator's true
        actions (they are assumed unavailable) and instead run each demonstration
        transition $(s_t, s_{t+1})$ through the inverse dynamics model, taking
        $\hat a_t = \arg\max_a M_\theta(a \mid s_t, s_{t+1})$. Feed the resulting
        $(s_t, \hat a_t)$ dataset into the existing \texttt{train\_policy} BC routine and
        evaluate as before.
\end{enumerate}

The policy network, the BC loss, and the evaluation loop stay exactly the same; what is
added is a second network plus its own data-collection and training stage, and the action
labels become \emph{predicted} rather than ground truth. The key practical risk is a
distribution mismatch: the inverse dynamics model is trained on states a random policy
visits (near the bottom of the valley) but is applied to states the expert visits (high up
on both hills), so its accuracy on the demonstration data can be much worse than its
training accuracy. (BCO($\alpha$) fixes this by alternating: roll out the current learned
policy, add those transitions to the inverse-dynamics training set, retrain, and re-label.
With $\alpha = 0$ we do a single pass.)




\section{Part 7}

\subsection{Question}
\begin{sloppypar}
Implement and test BCO(0) by modifying the starter code in
\texttt{mountain\_car\_bco.py}. This starter code is identical to the BC code,
except: (1) the function \texttt{collect\_random\_interaction\_data} has been
added to help with learning the inverse dynamics model; (2) a new argument for
number of inverse dynamics training iterations has been added; and (3) some
comments have been added about where to use the inverse dynamics model instead of
ground-truth actions. You may need to experiment with different neural network
sizes and training iterations to get the inverse dynamics model to work well.
Report how well it works and what you tried to get it to work, including stats on
the accuracy of prediction when tested on the demonstration data (training should
be done with the random interaction data, not the demonstrations).
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Solution}
\textbf{Implementation.} I implemented BCO(0) in \texttt{mountain\_car\_bco.py} along the
three stages above: an \texttt{InverseDynamicsNetwork} (input $(s_t,\; s_{t+1} - s_t)$,
3-way output) trained with full-batch Adam and cross-entropy on random interaction data
only, then used to relabel the demonstration transitions, which are fed to the unchanged BC
routine. The demonstrations are cached in \texttt{demos.pkl} so that every configuration is
scored on \emph{the same} $734$ demonstration transitions (5 demos: $196$ accelerate-left,
$110$ no-op, $428$ accelerate-right), and each run appends a row of config $+$ metrics to a
CSV. \texttt{run\_p7.sh} sweeps network size and inverse dynamics training iterations with
$5$ seeds per configuration and $20$ evaluation episodes per run
(\texttt{results\_p7.csv}); \texttt{run\_p7\_coverage.sh} is a second sweep, over the amount and
coverage of the random interaction data, written to
\texttt{results\_p7\_coverage.csv}. Every number below is a mean
over the $5$ seeds.

\begin{sloppypar}
\noindent The only change to the provided \texttt{collect\_random\_interaction\_data} is an
optional \texttt{action\_repeat} argument (the default of $1$ reproduces the original):
every action is still drawn uniformly at random, and no demonstration information is used.
Runs use seeds $0$--$4$ (\texttt{-{}-seed}), which control PyTorch, NumPy and the
environment's resets and action sampling; evaluation episode $i$ starts from
\texttt{env.reset(seed=1000+i)}, so every configuration is scored on the same $20$ start
states. The exact commands are in \texttt{run\_p7.sh} in the repository.
\end{sloppypar}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Accuracy on the demonstration data.} Training accuracy on the random
interaction data is $1.000$ in every configuration except the smallest network, so it is not
a useful diagnostic; the informative number is accuracy on the demonstration transitions,
which the model never trains on. A training accuracy of $1.000$ is expected: MountainCar's
dynamics are deterministic, $v_{t+1} = v_t + 0.001\,(a_t - 1) - 0.0025\cos(3p_t)$ (before
clipping), so $(v_{t+1} - v_t + 0.0025\cos(3p_t))/0.001 = a_t - 1$ identifies the action
exactly from $(s_t, s_{t+1})$. The only exceptions are collisions with the left wall at
$p=-1.2$, where the velocity is reset to $0$, and (rarely) steps where the velocity hits its
$\pm 0.07$ limit. In the baseline configuration ($64$ units, $2$ hidden
layers, $1000$ iterations, $5$ random episodes $=1000$ transitions) demonstration accuracy
is only $0.771$, and the resulting policy reaches the flag in $36\%$ of evaluation episodes
at an average return of $-185.2$.

Extra capacity was useless or harmful. Only width $2$ genuinely underfits, and it is the

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Where the errors are.} Breaking the baseline's demonstration accuracy down
shows what the missing $23\%$ is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{By action:} no-op is predicted at $0.933$, accelerate-left at $0.755$, and
        accelerate-right --- the most common expert action ($428$ of $734$ transitions) ---
        at only $0.736$, because the random policy rarely produces the sustained rightward
        motion the expert uses to climb.
  \item \emph{By state region:} a uniformly random policy hardly leaves the bottom of the
        valley, so the baseline model is trained on positions in roughly $[-0.74, -0.32]$
        but applied to demonstration states spanning $[-1.19, 0.50]$, leaving about $403$ of
        the $734$ demonstration transitions outside the covered position range. On
        demonstration states \emph{inside} the covered range accuracy is $0.935$; on those
        outside it, it falls to $0.637$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What I had to do to make the inverse dynamics model work.} Two things
outside network size and iteration count mattered far more than either of them.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{State coverage of the random data.} Holding each (still uniformly random)
        action for $k$ steps lets the car build momentum and extends the random data's
        position range dramatically at the \emph{same} budget of $1000$ transitions. With
        $k=8$ the range grows to $[-1.11, 0.11]$, out-of-range demonstration transitions
        drop from $403$ to $130$, demonstration accuracy rises to $0.924$, and the policy
        reaches the flag in $100\%$ of evaluation episodes at an average return of $-140.5$.
        Simply collecting more uniformly random episodes is far less efficient: $200$
        episodes ($40{,}000$ transitions, $40\times$ the data) reach $0.849$ accuracy, which
        action repeat $8$ beats with $1000$.
  \item \textbf{Input representation / normalization.} The raw features are on very
        different scales: position lives in $[-1.2, 0.6]$, while a single action changes
        velocity by only $\pm 0.001$. I feed $(s_t,\; s_{t+1} - s_t)$, i.e.\ the state plus
        the \emph{delta}, and standardize all $4$ features using statistics computed from
        the random interaction data. Normalization is what makes the coverage fix pay off:
        at action repeat $8$, turning it off drops demonstration accuracy from $0.924$ to
        $0.824$ and accuracy on out-of-range states from $0.647$ to $0.305$. At the
        baseline's limited coverage, normalization actually hurt slightly ($0.771$
        normalized versus $0.818$ unnormalized), even though $331$ of the $734$
        demonstration states ($45\%$) lie inside the covered range. So its benefit is
        specific to the extended-coverage setting; with $5$ seeds, part of the baseline
        reversal may be seed variation.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{How well BCO(0) works.} The baseline configuration is poor: $36\%$ success
and an average return of $-185.2$. With the coverage fix the method works well: demonstration
labelling accuracy $0.924$, $20/20$ successful evaluation episodes for every seed, average
return $-140.5$, and the single best run in the whole sweep (action repeat $2$, seed $0$)
averaged $-120.9$ over its $20$ evaluation episodes with $100\%$ success and a best episode
of $-90$; the best individual episode anywhere in the sweep was $-86$. These best-case
numbers were selected after the fact, so they are upper bounds rather than expected results.
For reference, BC trained on the \emph{true} actions in Part 4 averaged $-145.8$ and my own
best demonstration scored $-87$, but the Part 4 number is not directly comparable: it used a
different set of demonstrations and $6$ unseeded evaluation episodes, whereas the numbers
here are $5$-seed means over $20$ fixed episodes on the $734$-transition demonstration set.
Variance across seeds is large throughout
(the same configuration can go from $0\%$ to $100\%$ success), which is why every number
reported here is a $5$-seed mean rather than a single run.

So BCO(0) does solve MountainCar, but its performance is governed almost entirely by whether
the self-supervised interaction data covers the states the demonstrator visits, and only
weakly (and non-monotonically) by the size of the inverse dynamics network or how long it
trains. This is consistent with the paper's motivation for the post-demonstration iterations
of BCO($\alpha$), which address exactly this mismatch by adding on-policy transitions from
the states the learner actually reaches.
