\section{Part 1}

\subsection{Question}
Run the following code again
\begin{verbatim}
python test_gym.py
\end{verbatim}
What do you notice happening? What specifically about MountainCar makes the
problem difficult for RL?

\subsection{Solution}
The random policy jitters the car back and forth near the bottom of the
valley and essentially never leaves it: every one of the 200 steps returns $-1$ and the
episode ends with a return of $-200.0$, with the car still in the basin.

MountainCar is hard for RL because the reward signal carries no information about
progress. The engine is weaker than gravity, so the only way to reach the flag is to
first drive \emph{away} from it and pump energy into the system over many steps
(a long, precisely correlated sequence of actions). A uniformly random policy has
essentially zero probability of producing such a sequence within 200 steps, so the
agent almost never sees a non $-200$ return. This means:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exploration / sparse-in-practice reward:} all trajectories look equally
        bad ($-200$), so the reward gives no gradient toward the goal. The agent is
        stuck in a flat region of the return landscape, i.e. a local optimum where
        nothing it tries locally looks better than anything else.
  \item \textbf{Long credit-assignment horizon:} the action that matters (backing up the
        left hill) is rewarded only tens of steps later, when the car finally clears the
        right hill.
  \item \textbf{Counter-intuitive optimal behavior:} a greedy/myopic policy that pushes
        toward the goal never succeeds; the optimal policy must temporarily move in the
        wrong direction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Part 5}

\subsection{Question}
What do you think will happen if we give good and bad demonstrations?
You will now give two demonstrations. For the first one, just press the right
arrow key for the entire episode until it restarts. Then for the second demo,
give a good demonstration that quickly gets out of the valley.
\begin{verbatim}
python mountain_car_bc.py --num_demos 2
\end{verbatim}
What does the policy learn? Why might bad demonstrations be a problem? Briefly
suggest one potential idea for making BC robust to bad demonstrations, as long as
they are a minority of demonstrations.

\subsection{Solution}
I expected the bad demonstration to pull the policy toward always
accelerating right, and that is essentially what happened, but the failure was even more
complete than that. With one all-right demonstration ($-200.0$) and one good demonstration
($-200.0$ as recorded for that episode), the learned policy never escapes the valley.
All six evaluation episodes returned $-200.0$, so the average, minimum and maximum
returns are all $-200.0$.

BC loss & $1.2432$ & $0.9600$ & $0.7796$ & $0.6149$ & $0.5974$ & $0.5985$ & $0.5966$ & $0.5936$ & $0.5924$ & $0.5921$ & $0.5917$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The BC loss also plateaus at $\approx 0.59$ after about 40 iterations and stops improving,
which is the signature of \emph{irreducible label noise}: the two demonstrations visit
overlapping states but label them with different actions, so no deterministic policy can
fit both. Cross-entropy responds by predicting something close to the state-conditional
average of the two behaviors, and because the argmax of that average is dominated by the
``push right'' action (which appears in both the bad demo and parts of the good one), the
policy just pushes right and never builds momentum. Averaging two valid-but-different
behaviors gives behavior that is worse than either.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Why bad demonstrations are a problem.} BC is pure supervised learning on
$(s,a)$ pairs and has no notion of demonstration quality: every transition contributes
equally to the loss, so a bad demonstrator is imitated just as faithfully as a good one.
Worse, in MountainCar the good and bad policies disagree precisely in the states that
matter (when to switch direction), so the conflict is not averaged away harmlessly, and it
destroys the one behavior that solves the task.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{One idea for robustness.} Weight demonstrations rather than treating them
uniformly: since we can evaluate each demonstration's return in the environment (or its
episode length / whether it reached the flag), fit BC with per-demonstration weights
$w_i \propto \exp(R_i / \beta)$, or simply filter to the top-$k$ / above-median
demonstrations before training. Because bad demos are assumed to be a minority, the median
return is a good threshold and the outliers get (near-)zero weight. If no reward is
available at all, an unsupervised variant works similarly: fit BC, then compute each
transition's loss under the fitted policy and down-weight or drop the demonstrations with
the highest disagreement (a trimmed-loss / RANSAC-style approach), iterating a few times so
that the majority mode wins and the inconsistent minority is discarded.




\section{Part 6}

\subsection{Question}
At a conceptual level, describe what changes you would need to make to the BC
code to implement BCO(0), as described in the paper we read in class:
\url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.01954.pdf}. Answer this question before starting
Part 7.

\subsection{Solution}
BCO(0) removes the assumption that the demonstrations contain actions: we
observe only the state sequences $\{(s_t, s_{t+1})\}$ of the demonstrator and must infer
the actions ourselves before we can run BC. Conceptually there are three changes to the BC
code, and one full pass through them ($\alpha = 0$ means no post-demonstration iteration):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pre-demonstration / self-supervised data collection.} Before touching the
        demonstrations, interact with the environment using a random policy and record
        $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ transitions. Here the actions \emph{are} known because we
        chose them, so this data is free and needs no human. This is what
        \texttt{collect\_random\_interaction\_data} provides.

  \item \textbf{Learn an inverse dynamics model.} Train a new network
        $M_\theta(a \mid s_t, s_{t+1})$ on the random interaction data. Since the action
        space is discrete (3 actions), this is again a classification problem trained with
        cross-entropy, structurally the same training loop as BC, but with input
        $(s_t, s_{t+1})$ (i.e.\ 4 input features instead of 2) and the executed action as
        the label. This model is trained \emph{only} on the self-generated data, never on
        the demonstrations.

  \item \textbf{Label the demonstrations, then clone.} Discard the demonstrator's true
        actions (they are assumed unavailable) and instead run each demonstration
        transition $(s_t, s_{t+1})$ through the inverse dynamics model, taking
        $\hat a_t = \arg\max_a M_\theta(a \mid s_t, s_{t+1})$. Feed the resulting
        $(s_t, \hat a_t)$ dataset into the existing \texttt{train\_policy} BC routine and
        evaluate as before.
\end{enumerate}

So the policy network, the BC loss, and the evaluation loop stay exactly the same; what is
added is a second network plus its own data-collection and training stage, and the action
labels become \emph{predicted} rather than ground truth. The key practical risk is a
distribution mismatch: the inverse dynamics model is trained on states a random policy
visits (near the bottom of the valley) but is applied to states the expert visits (high up
on both hills), so its accuracy on the demonstration data can be much worse than its
training accuracy. (BCO($\alpha$) fixes this by alternating: roll out the current learned
policy, add those transitions to the inverse-dynamics training set, retrain, and re-label.
With $\alpha = 0$ we do a single pass.)




\section{Part 7}

\subsection{Question}
\begin{sloppypar}
Implement and test BCO(0) by modifying the starter code in
\texttt{mountain\_car\_bco.py}. This starter code is identical to the BC code,
except: (1) the function \texttt{collect\_random\_interaction\_data} has been
added to help with learning the inverse dynamics model; (2) a new argument for
number of inverse dynamics training iterations has been added; and (3) some
comments have been added about where to use the inverse dynamics model instead of
ground-truth actions. You may need to experiment with different neural network
sizes and training iterations to get the inverse dynamics model to work well.
Report how well it works and what you tried to get it to work, including stats on
the accuracy of prediction when tested on the demonstration data (training should
be done with the random interaction data, not the demonstrations).
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Solution}
\textbf{Implementation.} I implemented BCO(0) in
\texttt{mountain\_car\_bco.py} exactly along the three stages above: an
\texttt{InverseDynamicsNetwork} (input $(s_t, s_{t+1})$, 3-way output) trained with
full-batch Adam and cross-entropy on random interaction data, then used to relabel the
demonstration transitions, which are then fed to the unchanged BC routine. The
demonstrations are cached in \texttt{demos.pkl} so that every configuration is compared on
\emph{the same} 249 demonstration transitions (5 demos), and each run appends a row of
config $+$ metrics to a CSV. I ran a sweep of 96 runs (\texttt{run\_p7.sh},
\texttt{results\_p7.csv}) over hidden width, depth, learning rate, number of inverse
dynamics iterations, number of BC iterations, amount of random data, input normalization,
and action repeat, with 3 seeds per configuration.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What I had to do to make the inverse dynamics model work.} Two changes
mattered far more than network size:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Input representation / normalization.} The raw features are on wildly
        different scales: position lives in $[-1.2, 0.6]$, while a single action changes
        velocity by only $\pm 0.001$. I therefore feed
        $(s_t,\; s_{t+1} - s_t)$ (position/velocity plus the \emph{delta}) and standardize
        all 4 features using statistics computed from the random interaction data. Without
        this the action-identifying signal is numerically buried and training is much
        slower and less reliable.
  \item \textbf{State coverage of the random data (the real bottleneck).} A uniformly
        random policy hardly leaves the bottom of the valley, so the model is trained on
        positions in roughly $[-0.73, -0.31]$ but applied to demonstration states spanning
        $[-0.94, 0.50]$. Adding an \texttt{--action\_repeat} option, holding each
        (still uniformly random) action for $k$ steps so the car builds momentum and
        covers far more of the track, extends the random-data range to roughly
        $[-1.2, 0.48]$ and essentially eliminates the mismatch.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Accuracy results.} Training accuracy on the random interaction data is
almost always $1.000$, so it is not a useful diagnostic; the informative number is
accuracy on the (held-out) demonstration transitions. Means over 3 seeds:

The best individual runs reached $100\%$ demonstration-labelling accuracy and returns of
$-147$ to $-150$ (with up to $6/6$ successful evaluation episodes and a best single episode
of $-122$), i.e.\ roughly comparable to the BC-with-true-actions result of Part 4
($-145.8$), which is the natural upper bound for BCO(0) here.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Where the errors are.} Breaking the baseline's demonstration accuracy down
confirms the coverage diagnosis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{By action:} accelerate-left and no-op are predicted at $100\%$, while
        accelerate-right drops to $\approx 0.82$, because the random policy rarely produces the
        sustained rightward motion the expert uses to climb.
  \item \emph{By state region:} on demonstration states \emph{inside} the position range
        covered by the random data, accuracy is $1.00$; on states \emph{outside} it,
        accuracy falls to $\approx 0.79$. With action repeat, almost all demonstration
        states fall inside the covered range and accuracy goes to $1.00$ in both buckets.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Things that did \emph{not} help.} Width and depth were largely irrelevant
once normalization was in place. Hidden width $4$ already gives $\approx 0.98$
demonstration accuracy, and width $64$ with 3 or 4 layers is no better (mean returns
$-189.5$ for the width sweep, $-192.3$ for the depth sweep). Training the inverse dynamics
model \emph{longer} was mildly harmful (\texttt{iters} sweep: mean demo accuracy $0.789$,
mean return $-195.0$): it overfits the narrow random-data distribution and generalizes
worse to expert states. Likewise, more BC iterations ($500$/$1000$ instead of $100$) hurt
(mean return $-193.8$), since the policy then fits the noisy \emph{predicted} labels too
tightly. Variance across seeds is large throughout, since the same configuration can go from
$0\%$ to $100\%$ success, so all conclusions above are drawn from the multi-seed means
rather than single runs.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Summary.} BCO(0) works on MountainCar, but its performance is governed
almost entirely by whether the self-supervised interaction data covers the states the
demonstrator visits. Fixing the input representation and broadening random-policy coverage
(action repeat $+$ more episodes) takes demonstration-labelling accuracy from $\approx 87\%$
to $\approx 100\%$ and the success rate from $0.39$ to $0.67$, recovering close to the
performance of BC with ground-truth actions, consistent with the paper's motivation for
the post-demonstration iterations of BCO($\alpha$), which address exactly this mismatch.
